Four problems you can play with

Each one below is an open problem in mathematics, small enough to try by hand. After you have tried it, the page says what has now been proved. Every proof comes with files and programs that let anyone check it: see the repository or the plain-language guide.

Hadwiger's covering problem · posed 1957

Cover a shape with smaller copies of itself

Drag the coloured copies onto the shape until no red is left. Each copy is the same shape, shrunk. You may slide it but not turn it. Try the square with 3 copies, then with 4.

A copy that is even slightly smaller can reach only one corner of the square at a time, and there are four corners. In the plane that is the whole story: every convex shape needs at most 4 copies, and only parallelograms need all 4.

The open problem is the same question for solid shapes. A cube needs 8 copies, one per corner, and the conjecture says 8 are always enough. Nobody can prove 8. The number that can be proved has come down slowly.

Result. Every convex body in three-dimensional space can be covered by 13 smaller copies of itself. The proof is about 7.5 million exact calculations, each stored and checked by two independent programs. Proof and checkers

Heptagon packing · pattern found 1990

Pack seven-sided tiles as tightly as you can

Regular heptagons cannot tile a floor without gaps. Squeeze this pattern together and watch how much of the floor it covers. It locks when the tiles touch.

The pattern uses the tile two ways up. Fully squeezed it covers 89.27% of the plane. It was long conjectured that nothing does better; the same question for five-sided tiles was settled in 2016.

Result. No arrangement of congruent regular heptagons covers more of the plane than this pattern. The proof is computer-assisted, about 125 million cases. It has not yet been formally verified or read by a specialist, and the repository says so. Proof and caveats

The Collatz problem · open since 1937

Halve it if even, triple it and add one if odd

Pick a starting number and follow the rule. Every number anyone has tried ends at 1. Nobody can prove that it always does.

The line shows the size of the number at each step, on a compressed scale so that large values fit. Each dot is a low point, where the number stops falling and starts to climb again.

One way the conjecture could fail is a loop: a number that eventually comes back to itself. If a loop exists, how many low points must it have?

Result. A Collatz loop other than 1, 4, 2 has at least 112 low points. This does not solve the problem. It shows how complicated a counterexample of this kind would have to be. The proof is checked line by line by the proof assistant Lean. Proof

The lonely runner conjecture · 1967

Is every runner alone at some moment?

Runners circle a track, each at a steady speed of their own. The conjecture says each runner is at some moment lonely: at least one lap divided by the number of runners away from everyone else. Watch the runner standing at the top. Only differences in speed matter, so that runner can stand still.

In a hardest case the runner becomes lonely only just: the gap reaches the required distance exactly and never goes beyond it.

Result. The hardest cases with 6 moving runners and with 7 moving runners are now completely listed: one for 6 (speeds 1 to 6) and three for 7, among them the 8-runner example above. This answers a question posed by Perarnau and Serra. Proof
